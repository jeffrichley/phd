## What it establishes

Where Lighthill supplies the theory, this paper supplies the high-resolution computational
confirmation and, crucially, isolates anguilliform swimming specifically. Using
self-propelled, wall-resolved large-eddy simulation with a curvilinear immersed-boundary
method and implicitly coupled fluid–structure interaction, the authors show that pressure
around anguilliform swimmers scales with theoretical fluid acceleration, indicating that
"the main mechanism for propulsion in anguilliform swimmers is added mass (unsteady),"
while carangiform swimmers show both added-mass and lift-based (steady) contributions.

## What to pull

- The asymmetry that makes the dissertation's embodiment choice bite: omitting or lagging
  lateral added mass is first-order for an eel-form robot, because added mass is the
  propulsion mechanism itself, while it is more forgivable for a tuna-like robot where
  lift shares the load. This is why the simulator fidelity gap matters most for exactly
  the body this dissertation studies (RQ1).
- The pairing rule: cite this alongside Lighthill (1971) so the "added mass carries the
  thrust" claim rests on theory and current CFD together.

## Where it stops

The study is scoped to the inertial regime, so the Reynolds-number qualifier is mandatory
when leaning on it: at low Reynolds number resistive forces dominate and the fitted
added-mass coefficient goes to zero. It also concerns biological swimmers resolved by CFD,
not robots trained in GPU learning simulators: it establishes what the physics is, not
whether any learning platform gets that physics right, which is the dissertation's first
question. One reconciliation to honour in writing: the platform's own steady-cruise
force-balance number is a statement about steady cruise, not about the reactive-thrust
mechanism during undulation, and must be framed that way to avoid reading as
self-contradiction.
