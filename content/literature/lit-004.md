## What it establishes

This is the citation behind the claim that lateral added mass is first-order for
anguilliform locomotion, and the paper the dissertation's hydrodynamics plugin is named
for. Lighthill extends elongated-body theory so that "a prediction of instantaneous
reactive force between fish and water is obtained for fish motions of arbitrary amplitude."
His reactive force ("forces resulting from the inertia of associated water movements")
is added mass in his own words; the paper's own terms are "reactive force" and "virtual
mass," and it never uses the phrase "added mass" at all. The large-amplitude version
matters: a real undulating robot operates at large amplitude, and citing the 1960
small-amplitude note for a large-amplitude claim is a known reviewer objection.

## What to pull

- The identity at the heart of the embodiment argument: the reactive thrust of undulatory
  swimming is the inertia of the water moved laterally by the body, so the measured
  lateral added-mass factor is the propulsion term itself, not fluid coupling in the
  abstract (bears on RQ1's motivation and RQ2's premise that gait modes are distinct
  regimes).
- The correct edition of the theory to cite for a large-amplitude swimmer: 1971, not 1960.
- Its terminology, when quoting: search this paper for "virtual mass," not "added mass":
  the zero hits on the latter are vocabulary, not absence.

## Where it stops

Lighthill gives the theory and nothing downstream of it: no simulation, no robot, no
measurement of whether a given physics engine realizes the reactive force. The
dissertation pairs it with modern wall-resolved CFD (Daghooghi et al. 2025) so the
mechanism claim rests on theory plus current computation rather than a 1971 paper alone,
and scopes it with the mandatory Reynolds-number qualifier: added-mass dominance is an
inertial-regime statement, and at low Reynolds number resistive forces dominate instead.
