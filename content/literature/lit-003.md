## What it establishes

Lamb's treatment of the translation and rotation of an ellipsoid in liquid (§§114–115 of
the sixth edition) gives the closed-form inertia coefficients from which the dissertation's
analytic targets are computed. For the platform's prolate-ellipsoid geometry (semi-axes
0.075 × 0.04 × 0.04 m, 1 kg, neutrally buoyant in water), the closed form gives an axial
inertia coefficient of about 0.115 and a lateral coefficient of about 0.345, effective-mass
ratios of 1.115 axial and 1.345 lateral. The lateral value being three times the axial
value is the anisotropy the whole embodiment argument rests on.

## What to pull

- The headline validation numbers (1.115 axial, 1.345 lateral) with first-principles
  provenance: they are computed from Lamb's coefficient integrals in an archived script,
  not copied from a table, and are therefore defensible even if a reviewer disputes an
  edition or section (RQ1).
- Three independent corroborations agree: the closed-form derivation, Brennen's 1982 review
  of added mass (the accessible reference marine-robotics papers actually reach for, which
  tabulates the same prolate-ellipsoid values and cites Lamb), and MuJoCo's own
  documentation, which derives the same coefficients from the same chapter.
- The verified citation location: §§114–115, p. 152, confirmed against the sixth-edition
  scan's table of contents. An earlier "Art. 373" location was a memory guess and is wrong.

## Where it stops

Lamb supplies theory, not measurement: the coefficients say what a correct potential-flow
implementation must produce, and nothing about what any given simulator actually produces.
That comparison is the dissertation's to make. The theory is also regime-bound: added-mass
dominance is an inertial-regime statement, and at low Reynolds number resistive forces win
and the fitted added-mass coefficient goes to zero, so every claim built on these numbers
must state the robot's Reynolds number.
