## What it establishes

This paper separates a second failure mode of continual learning from catastrophic
forgetting: loss of plasticity, the decay of the ability to learn from new data at all.
On a sequence of 2000 binary classification tasks drawn from ImageNet, well-tuned deep
networks improve at first and then fall to or below the level of a linear network; the
result is robust across architectures, step sizes, network widths, and rates of
distribution change, and is replicated on 800 permuted-MNIST tasks and an idealized
regression problem covering six activation functions. The mechanism is that the benefits
of random initialization (small weights, diverse and non-saturated units) exist only at
the start of training and are consumed as training proceeds: plasticity loss tracks
growing weight magnitude, an increasing fraction of dead units, and a falling effective
rank of the representation. Common tools make it worse (Adam catastrophically, dropout
and normalization moderately), while L2 regularization and shrink-and-perturb ease it only
within narrow hyperparameter ranges. The proposed cure, continual backpropagation,
reinitializes a tiny fraction of low-utility hidden units at every step and maintains
performance indefinitely, with accuracy on the 5000th task exceeding the first.

## What to pull

- The two-failure-mode framing: in-weights lifelong learning must fight forgetting of old
  skills and loss of plasticity for new ones simultaneously: an external store of frozen
  skills with a small, plasticity-managed acquisition component sidesteps both by
  construction, which is the dissertation's motivating argument in its sharpest form.
- The Adam result: the default optimizer of deep reinforcement learning shows the most
  catastrophic plasticity loss, and reinforcement learning is inherently continual because
  the policy keeps changing, a warning that applies directly to long skill-sequence
  baselines.
- Baseline hygiene: any fine-tuning baseline run across many sequential skills should
  control for plasticity loss (L2, shrink-and-perturb, or continual backpropagation), or
  its late-sequence failures confound the comparison.
- A likely explanation for the second-pass forward-transfer decay observed in the
  Continual World benchmark, letting the two sources be cited as one argument.

## Where it stops

The systematic evidence is entirely supervised: sequential image classification and
regression in which nonstationarity is manufactured by reshuffling class pairs or
permuting pixels, so consecutive tasks are deliberately unrelated and nothing
distinguishes a small parametric change from a categorical regime shift, the distinction
that fluid coupling imposes on a swimming body. There is no embodiment, no closed-loop
control, and no locomotion; the reinforcement-learning evidence in this work is a
preliminary appendix experiment rather than a systematic study. And the remedy operates
entirely inside the weights of one network: continual backpropagation keeps a learner
plastic but stores nothing, retrieves nothing, and says nothing about forward transfer
between related skills, so the question of whether retained skills can scaffold new
acquisition is untouched.
