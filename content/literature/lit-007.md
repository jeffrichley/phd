## What it establishes

LEGION couples a Dirichlet-process mixture model (which infers the task and grows a new
cluster component per new task, with no predefined task count) to a soft actor-critic
policy conditioned on the inferred knowledge, using language embeddings as
side-information for task inference. On a real KUKA iiwa arm over ten manipulation tasks
it reaches average success 0.84 with forgetting 0.0 and forward transfer 0.10 (against a
multi-task upper bound of 0.94, five seeds), and solves a long-horizon "clean the table"
mission of seven subtasks by recombining knowledge from the stream. Its replay-based
baselines (Reservoir, Perfect Memory, A-GEM) flatten at around 0.2 while LEGION climbs:
direct evidence that replay-based continual learning underperforms on robots.

## What to pull

- The existence proof RQ2 leans on: preserving and recombining skills for novel
  long-horizon tasks on a real robot is achievable (0.84 success with zero forgetting),
  which de-risks the dissertation's ambition.
- Evidence that replay and weight-update continual learning are brittle on robots, from
  its own baseline comparisons.
- The evaluation template worth borrowing: forgetting and forward-transfer metrics, five
  or more seeds, easy-to-hard task ordering, and the two-loop knowledge-recall probe in
  which revisited tasks merge into existing clusters rather than spawning new ones.

## Where it stops

LEGION's knowledge space is non-parametric only in the Bayesian sense of unbounded
clusters: it is still a generative model whose parameters are trained, not a frozen
policy consulting an external, inspectable, retrieved memory. The embodiment is a
fixed-base manipulator, so no fluid coupling and no locomotion regimes are involved, and
the study never runs the controlled comparison of external-memory reuse against
weight-update continual learning on the same tasks. Those three gaps (mechanism,
embodiment, and comparison) are what the dissertation's second contribution fills.
