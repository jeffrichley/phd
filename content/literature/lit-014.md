## What it establishes

This is the classical counterpoint to Lighthill's reactive theory, the other pole of
undulatory propulsion. Taylor idealizes a swimming snake, eel, or worm as a flexible
cylinder carrying a traveling wave of bending, and assumes "the force of each element of
the cylinder … to be the same as that which would act on a corresponding element of a
long straight cylinder moving at the same speed and inclination." Normal drag scales with
sin² of the local incidence; the longitudinal skin friction, which Taylor calls "of
paramount importance," opposes it; equilibrium over one wavelength yields a swimming
diagram relating amplitude-to-wavelength ratio, slip ratio V/U, and Reynolds number. The
theory places James Gray's photographed snake (measured V/U = 0.7, predicted 0.55) and
leech roughly correctly, shows the snake's amplitude is near the minimum-energy wave, and
predicts, before the animal was found, that a suitably rough body can swim by sending
waves forward, which Gray's photographs of the marine worm *Nereis diversicolor* confirm.

## What to pull

- The named counterpoint for the regime qualifier on RQ1: added-mass dominance is an
  inertial-regime statement, and Taylor's resistive theory is what governs instead at low
  Reynolds number or for rough slender bodies. Citing the counterpoint by name makes the
  qualifier scholarly rather than defensive.
- The lineage of the drag terms: Taylor's quasi-steady normal/tangential decomposition is
  the ancestor of the cross-flow drag coefficients in modern underwater-snake models and
  of the velocity-dependent damping the dissertation's hydrodynamics plugin carries. Only
  the acceleration reaction lies outside his framework.
- The framing sentence for related work: undulatory propulsion has two classical poles,
  Taylor's resistive (velocity-based) and Lighthill's reactive (inertia-based), and which
  dominates is a Reynolds-number and slenderness question, not a stylistic choice.

## Where it stops

Taylor builds the theory from oblique-cylinder wind-tunnel data and quasi-steady force
balance; fluid inertia enters only through drag coefficients, never as a reaction to
acceleration. He scopes it himself: the straight-cylinder assumption is "likely to be
inaccurate either when the diameter of the cylinder is comparable with the wave-length of
the motion or when the Reynolds number associated with it is very low," and the
head-to-tail amplitude growth of real swimmers is left untreated. A 1952 analytic theory
checked against photographs, it offers no computation, no robot, and no bearing on how a
physics engine realizes forces it does not contain.
