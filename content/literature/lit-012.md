## What it establishes

This is the landmark demonstration that a central pattern generator (a network of coupled
oscillators that produces rhythmic output without rhythmic input, the biological circuit
behind locomotion) is a workable controller for a real multi-jointed robot. A salamander
robot with an actuated spine and four limbs, driven by a chain of body oscillators plus
four limb oscillators, swims with a head-to-tail travelling wave and walks with an S-shaped
standing wave, both commanded by nothing more than a single drive signal standing in for
brainstem stimulation. The gait switch is emergent: limb oscillators saturate and fall
silent at high drive, releasing the faster body rhythm, which reproduces the abrupt
walk-to-swim transition seen when the animal's brainstem is stimulated. The paper's closing
claim is the one the field adopted: oscillator-based control reduces the dimensionality of
locomotion control while staying continuously adjustable in speed, heading, and gait.

## What to pull

- The clearest statement that a low-dimensional, drive-parameterised oscillator bank is the
  standard substrate for undulatory locomotion: the thing a stored locomotion skill on
  such a substrate actually is (bears on RQ2's choice of skill representation).
- The categorical-regime evidence: walking and swimming occupy non-overlapping frequency
  ranges with a gap between them, in both model and animal, and the transition is abrupt:
  gait modes behave as discrete regimes, not points on one continuous axis.
- The interface convention the dissertation inherits: one scalar drive for speed and gait,
  left–right drive asymmetry for turning.

## Where it stops

Every parameter (coupling weights, phase biases, intrinsic frequencies, the saturation
thresholds that make the switch happen) is set by the modeller, so the two-gait repertoire
is closed and the drive merely chooses within it. The swimming result is validated
empirically in a pool, but the controller itself contains no hydrodynamic term and no
sensory feedback: it is open-loop with respect to the fluid. What happens when the fluid
coupling changes, when a new gait must be acquired, or when a skill learned in one regime
is reused in another is entirely outside its scope: that is the assumption the
dissertation breaks by treating the oscillator substrate as shared and asking what
transfers across fluid-coupling regimes.
